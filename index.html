💪 6:30 PM

Okay, Let's Start

We sat down at our seats, opened our laptops, arranged everything and started preparing.

At this point, we were still fresh. We were talking about what we wanted to build, how the game would work and how quickly we could finish it.

Looking back, the confidence we had at 6:30 PM was impressive. And completely unnecessary.

The idea was Meen Chanda, a fish-market haggling game where you don't simply type what you want to say. You actually talk. The game listens to your voice — things like volume, pitch and pace — and uses that to affect the negotiation.

"We decided to make a game where arguing with a fish seller is a legitimate skill."

🐟 What We Built

Meen Chanda — The Fish Market Haggling Simulator

A voice-driven browser game where you haggle with a Kerala fish seller. Instead of typing, you actually speak — and the game listens to your voice to determine the negotiation outcome.

How It Works

Your browser captures your voice via the Web Audio API and analyses three acoustic parameters in real time:

  • Volume (RMS loudness) — How loud you're speaking
  • Pitch (fundamental frequency) — How high or panicked your voice sounds
  • Speaking pace — How fast you're rattling off words

These three values are combined into a Haggle Aggression Score. The higher your aggression, the more the fish seller raises the price. Stay calm, speak softly, and the price drops.

⚡ The Fish Market Axiom: The customer who screams louder gets charged more. The uncle who speaks calmly walks away with the best fish at the best price.

Tech Stack

  • Sarvam AI — Powers the fish seller's Malayalam dialogue and personality responses
  • Web Audio API — Client-side volume and pitch analysis via AnalyserNode (2048-point FFT)
  • Web Speech API — Speaking pace detection
  • Vanilla JavaScript — Game state machine, UI and all logic — zero backend, zero npm dependencies

🧠 What We Learned

We learned how difficult it is to turn a simple idea into something that actually works.

We learned that debugging at 7 PM and debugging at 4 AM are two completely different experiences.

We learned that food can restore motivation faster than any motivational speech.

We learned that a five-minute break can sometimes save you from staring at the same error for another hour.

We learned that looking at other people's projects is a great way to get ideas — and also a great way to realise everyone else is struggling too.

And most importantly, we learned that when you're tired enough, even a tiny bug feels personally offensive.
